Neural Circuit Architectural Priors for Rat Locomotion

Code

Abstract

Animals generate behavior through continuous interactions between their neural circuits, bodies, and environment. Embodied simulations provide a means to study these interactions by testing neural circuit models in biologically realistic settings. Recent efforts have developed embodied simulations of diverse animals, and emerging work on biologically grounded neural architectures has begun to integrate circuit connectivity and single-unit dynamics from experimental data into artificial neural networks, introducing stronger inductive biases and closer mechanistic alignment with biology. How can we develop embodied simulations that enable the study of increasingly complex bodies and neural circuits? In this work, we address the challenge through coordinated advances in the simulated body, neural architecture, and training algorithm. We introduce Rat v2, a biomechanical model that provides an efficient and flexible tool for simulating rat behavior. To control this body, we develop Rat NCAP, a biologically grounded neural architecture for locomotion based on mammalian spinal circuits that incorporates cell-type-specific connectivity and a novel flexor-extensor neuromechanical interface. To optimize circuit parameters, we refine an evolutionary algorithm to support reliable and efficient training. The integrated system learns to locomote at different speeds without imitation learning, producing locomotion statistics and gaits that match experimental data. Systematic analyses reveal that neuromechanical interface choice substantially affects trainability and gait quality, and that specific neural populations play distinct roles in gait generation. Together, these findings support the view that embodied simulations at an intermediate level of abstraction can simultaneously reproduce complex behavior and yield mechanistic insights into the neural circuits that generate it.

1. Locomotion Behavior

Rat NCAP learns to stand, walk, trot, and bound within a single model, producing naturalistic stepping and speed-dependent gaits. Prior work in this rat relied on imitation learning to obtain naturalistic behavior [Aldarondo et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2025] or produced visibly unrealistic movement without it [Merel et al., 2019]. Rat NCAP achieves comparable naturalism using only simple velocity rewards, demonstrating the powerful inductive bias of biological neural circuits.

2. Gait Transitions

Increasing brainstem command within a single episode reveals distinct gait patterns at each speed, consistent with speed-dependent recruitment of distinct locomotor patterns. Notably, gait transitions are not trained and reflect zero-shot generalization.

3. Gait Shapes

The neuromechanical interface determines not only trainability, but also the qualitative character of learned gaits. Despite similar asymptotic reward, the flexor-extensor interface produces qualitatively different locomotion: it uses substantially more negative mechanical power than the other interfaces, storing and returning energy through its antagonistic spring structure analogous to tendon compliance. This compliance is visible in joint trajectories: position and position-stiffness produce sharp arcs with brief ground contact, while flexor-extensor produces rounded trajectories with extended stance as the paw deforms along the ground.

4. Cell-Type Ablations

A central advantage of a biologically grounded circuit model is the ability to perform targeted in silico ablations of identified cell types, which is an analysis not possible with black-box controllers. In rodents, genetic ablation of V0v excitatory neurons eliminates trot while leaving walk and bound intact, and further ablation of V0d inhibitory neurons removes all alternating gaits, leaving bound as the only coordinated pattern [Bellardita and Kiehn, 2015]. Rat NCAP replicates both phenotypes: V0v ablation selectively removes trot while preserving walk, and combined V0v and V0d ablation eliminates all alternating gaits, leaving only bound. These results validate the RG circuit's functional organization, supporting its use for generating experimentally testable predictions about gait impairments through targeted cell-type ablations.
